[ SOC Simulation Write-up · June 2026 ]

Phishing Defence & Malware Containment

Executive summary

AlertSOC114, malicious attachment detected
VerdictTrue positive, host contained
MalwareTrojan downloader via weaponised Office attachment
ExploitCVE-2017-11882, Office Equation Editor
Dwell time27 minutes, delivery to C2 callback
MITRE ATT&CKT1566.001, T1203, T1105, T1071.001

Detection and email analysis

The alert opened on a suspicious attachment, and the first question was narrow: delivered, or stopped at the gateway? The console confirmed delivery to a finance mailbox. The lure itself was thin, two lines with no personalisation or order reference, relying on the fact that an invoice reaching a finance user is unremarkable enough to open without thinking. The sending domain was the more interesting detail: a legitimate academic domain, which passes reputation checks that a freshly registered lookalike would fail, and is a large part of why the message reached the inbox at all.

Static analysis

Reputation checking returned 35 of 62 vendors flagging the file, labelled as a trojan downloader with exploit and executes-dropped-file behaviour tags. The CVE tag shaped everything that followed: a 2021 document exploiting a flaw patched in November 2017 is not targeting a novel weakness, it is targeting an unpatched estate. That reframed the incident from a detection problem to a patch management problem before a single log was queried, and it set the expectation that a second stage existed somewhere to go looking for.

Process and download activity

// proxy log, recipient host

Request     GET http://andaluciabeach.net/image/network.exe

Process     EQNEDT32.EXE

Parent      excel.exe

Action      Allowed

This one line carries most of the evidential weight. The Equation Editor binary exists to render formulas inside Office documents and has no legitimate reason to make outbound HTTP requests, let alone retrieve an executable. Seeing it spawned by Excel and reaching an external host is the exploit caught in the act, confirming user execution rather than inferring it. Two further gaps: the payload was served as an .exe under an /image/ path, and the proxy allowed an executable over plain HTTP from an uncategorised domain, a control failure independent of the phishing email.

Network correlation

The affected host established an outbound connection to a known command and control server on port 443. That port is the expected choice: TLS conceals the payload from inspection and outbound HTTPS is almost never blocked, so the traffic blends into ordinary browsing. Twenty seven minutes separated delivery from callback, consistent with the attachment being opened shortly after arrival. At that point the chain was evidenced end to end, with no step assumed: message delivered, document opened, exploit triggered, payload downloaded, channel established.

Response and containment

  • Host isolated while left powered on, cutting the C2 channel while preserving volatile evidence for forensic review.
  • Credential reset recommended: anything cached on a host with an active channel has to be treated as exposed.
  • Attachment hash, payload URL and C2 address documented for blocking at proxy, firewall and endpoint layers.
  • Mailbox sweep recommended, since one recipient in an alert rarely means one recipient in the campaign.
  • Urgent audit of Office versions across the estate, as the exposure is unlikely to be limited to one host.

Lessons learned

Process lineage was the fastest route to a verdict; the parent and child relationship between Excel and the Equation Editor said more in one line than the reputation score did. Layered controls also failed independently: the gateway allowed the attachment, the proxy allowed the executable, and the patch had been available for over three years. Each gap was survivable alone, but together they produced a compromise. The decisive evidence came from placing three ordinary log sources in chronological order rather than from any sophisticated query.

[ Incident Report · July 2026 ]

Applying the NIST CSF to a DoS Incident

Summary

At approximately 09:20, all internal network services stopped responding. Investigation showed the network was overwhelmed by a sustained flood of incoming ICMP packets, consistent with a denial of service attack. The team contained the event by blocking incoming ICMP traffic at the perimeter, taking non-critical services offline and prioritising restoration of critical services. Normal operations were disrupted for around two hours. The attacker exploited a firewall that had been left unconfigured, with no rate limiting or filtering in place.

Identify

A full audit of the network, affected systems and perimeter devices confirmed the root cause: an unconfigured firewall with no rules restricting inbound ICMP traffic. The affected assets were the internal network and every dependent service. No evidence of data access or exfiltration; the attack targeted availability, not confidentiality.

Protect

The firewall is now fully configured with a rule that rate-limits incoming ICMP packets, and source IP address verification checks for spoofed addresses. An IDS/IPS filters ICMP traffic with suspicious characteristics. Firewall configurations will be reviewed on a regular schedule and staff will receive secure configuration training.

Detect

Network monitoring software alerts on abnormal traffic patterns and ICMP volume spikes. Firewall logging has been enabled so inbound traffic can be baselined against normal daily patterns, and user account activity is tracked to flag unusual behaviour.

Respond

The plan for future incidents: isolate affected systems quickly, block offending traffic at the perimeter and shed non-critical services to preserve capacity. All incidents are reported to upper management with legal and regulatory notifications met where applicable, followed by structured post-incident analysis.

Recover

Recovery was staged: attack traffic stayed blocked while congestion cleared, critical services returned first, and non-critical services were restored last once monitoring confirmed stability. This forms the recovery playbook for future availability incidents.

Reflection: a single overlooked control, one unconfigured firewall, was enough to take the entire internal network offline for two hours. Availability attacks do not need sophisticated intrusion, only an unguarded entry point, so perimeter configuration reviews are now a standing programme item.

[ Incident Report · July 2026 ]

Brute Force Attack & Malicious Redirect

Scenario

A former employee of a recipe website brute-forced the administrative account by repeatedly entering known default passwords, embedded JavaScript that prompted visitors to download an executable disguised as a browser update, then changed the admin password. Customers who ran the file were redirected to a spoofed clone of the site containing malware, and reported slower machines to the helpdesk.

Investigation

The attack was replicated in a sandboxed virtual machine while capturing traffic with tcpdump. The trace showed the browser resolving the legitimate domain over DNS (port 52444 to the DNS server), establishing an HTTP connection on port 80 confirmed by the SYN handshake flags, then the file download. Minutes later the capture showed a second DNS resolution for the spoofed domain and a new HTTP connection from a fresh source port, confirming the downloaded file redirected users to an attacker-controlled site.

// Extract from the tcpdump trace narrative

14:18:32 · DNS A? query for the legitimate domain, answered 203.0.113.22

14:18:36 · TCP SYN / SYN-ACK to port 80, HTTP GET / HTTP/1.1

14:20:32 · New DNS A? query for the spoofed domain, answered 192.0.2.17

14:25:29 · New HTTP session to the spoofed site from source port 56378

Protocol identified

HTTP at the application layer. Both the legitimate and spoofed sites communicated over port 80, and the malicious file was transported to user machines over HTTP.

Recommendations

  • Enforce a password policy requiring longer passwords with special characters, numbers and capitals, replacing default credentials immediately.
  • Add multi factor authentication as a second layer of identity proof.
  • Limit login attempts to blunt brute force attacks at source.

[ Incident Report · July 2026 ]

Wireshark DNS Outage Investigation

Incident

Customers reported a "destination port unreachable" error when attempting to visit a website. Packet sniffing tests were conducted in Wireshark to investigate. Note: IP addresses and the domain in the working log are anonymised stand-ins so nothing traces back to a real environment.

The captured log

13:24:32.192571 IP 198.51.100.23.52444 > 192.0.2.44.domain: 35084+ A?
facebook-connect.net. (21)
13:24:36.098564 IP 192.0.2.44 > 198.51.100.23: ICMP 192.0.2.44
udp port 53 unreachable length 254

13:26:32.192571 IP 198.51.100.23.52444 > 192.0.2.44.domain: 35084+ A?
facebook-connect.net. (21)
13:27:15.934126 IP 192.0.2.44 > 198.51.100.23: ICMP 192.0.2.44
udp port 53 unreachable length 320

13:28:32.192571 IP 198.51.100.23.52444 > 192.0.2.44.domain: 35084+ A?
facebook-connect.net. (21)
13:28:50.022967 IP 192.0.2.44 > 198.51.100.23: ICMP 192.0.2.44
udp port 53 unreachable length 150

Reading the log

  1. The first two lines show the computer sending a request to the DNS server, asking for the IP address of the domain. This request travels as a UDP packet.
  2. The next two lines show the reply. The line starting with "ICMP 192.0.2.44" is the beginning of an error message: the UDP packet never made it to port 53 on the DNS server.
  3. Each line starts with a timestamp. The first, 13:24:32.192571, means 1:24 p.m. and 32.192571 seconds.
  4. After the timestamp come the source and destination IP addresses. The address left of the > symbol is where the packet came from (the analyst's machine, 198.51.100.23) and the address right of it is where it was headed (the DNS server, 192.0.2.44). For the ICMP error reply this is reversed, since the error is being sent back.
  5. Then comes the detail: 35084 is a query ID, a label matching the request with its reply. The plus sign means flags are attached, and "A?" marks a request for an A record, the DNS record type that maps a domain name to an IP address. "ICMP" on the reply lines marks an error message rather than a normal DNS response.
  6. The final part, "udp port 53 unreachable", is the key finding. Port 53 is the port used for DNS, and "unreachable" means nothing was listening there to answer.
  7. The rest of the log shows the same request retried twice more, hitting the identical error each time.

Analysis

The UDP protocol carried the DNS query to resolve the domain, and ICMP carried the error response back. Because port 53 is the port associated with DNS, the repeated "udp port 53 unreachable" errors isolate the fault to the DNS service itself: the request never reached a working DNS listener. The A? flag on each query confirms the client was performing standard A-record resolution when the failures occurred.

Conclusion and next steps

It is highly likely the DNS server is not responding, either down due to a successful denial of service attack or misconfigured. The next investigative step is to determine whether the DNS server itself is offline or whether traffic to port 53 is being blocked by the firewall.

Business email compromise attempt

Description · The accounts payable team at a mid-sized architecture firm flagged an email requesting an urgent change to a vendor's payment routing number. The message impersonated a known supplier and was investigated as a suspected business email compromise (BEC) attempt.

Tools used · Email header analyser (message trace with SPF, DKIM and DMARC results), domain age lookup (WHOIS).

The 5 W's · An external actor spoofed the domain of a long-standing HVAC supplier from a look-alike domain registered nine days earlier, with the digit "1" substituted for the letter "l". The email arrived at 8:47 AM in the accounts payable inbox on the corporate Microsoft 365 tenant and was flagged by the clerk nine minutes later. No payment was released, so no compromise beyond the phishing attempt itself occurred.

Additional notes

Header analysis: SPF = fail, DKIM = none. Domain registered via a privacy-proxy registrar 9 days prior.

Actions taken: blocked sender domain at the mail gateway, notified the real supplier out-of-band, added a payment-change verification step (call-back to a known number) to the AP procedure.

Open question: should look-alike domain monitoring be added for the top 20 vendors?

Ransomware via exposed RDP

Description · A file-encrypting ransomware variant was detected on two servers at a regional logistics company after an exposed remote desktop (RDP) port was brute-forced. The case covers containment through recovery.

Tools used · EDR alert console, firewall and VPN logs, offline backup restore procedure.

The 5 W's · An unattributed actor gained access via credential-stuffing against an internet-facing RDP endpoint (port 3389 left open on a warehouse management server). After roughly 40 minutes of repeated login attempts starting at 1:31 AM, the attacker authenticated with a weak local admin password, disabled the endpoint agent and deployed a payload that encrypted shared drives on two on-premises Windows servers. Encryption activity triggered an EDR alert at 2:14 AM. The port had been left exposed without MFA or IP allowlisting after a vendor's temporary remote-support setup was never rolled back.

Additional notes

Containment: isolated both hosts from the network, closed RDP at the perimeter firewall, rotated all local admin credentials.

Recovery: restored affected shares from the previous night's offline backup (4-hour data loss window). No ransom paid.

Follow-up: audit all firewall rules for other temporary vendor-access exceptions; require MFA on any remote administration path going forward.

Departing employee data removal

Description · A departing employee at a software company copied a customer contract archive to a personal USB drive three days before their resignation date. The case was reviewed for policy violation and closed out with process recommendations.

Tools used · DLP (data loss prevention) alert log, endpoint USB device history, HR offboarding checklist.

The 5 W's · A sales operations employee who had submitted their resignation but remained on payroll for a two-week transition period triggered a DLP rule with a bulk transfer of a shared drive folder containing signed customer contracts to an unregistered USB device at 4:05 PM, three business days before their last day, on a company-issued laptop on-site. USB storage was not blocked by policy for the role, and departing-employee monitoring was not yet in place until the final offboarding day.

Additional notes

Resolution: HR and legal were looped in; the employee confirmed and deleted the copy under supervision as a condition of a clean separation.

Process gap: departing-employee monitoring should begin at resignation notice, not on the last working day.

Recommendation: block removable storage by default for roles with access to customer contract data, with exceptions granted case-by-case.

Misconfigured cloud storage bucket

Description · A misconfigured cloud storage bucket belonging to a health-and-wellness app was found publicly listable by an external security researcher, who reported it through the company's disclosure programme before any confirmed misuse.

Tools used · Cloud provider access logs, bucket policy and IAM review, responsible-disclosure intake ticket.

The 5 W's · No malicious actor was identified. The exposure was traced to an internal engineering change that set a storage bucket's access policy to public during a migration, caught and reported responsibly before confirmed misuse.

Why this entry matters

Detection and analysis is not only about attackers. Configuration drift during routine engineering work is one of the most common causes of real-world data exposure.

The journal applies the same 5 W's discipline whether the cause is an adversary, an insider or an honest mistake.

File Permissions Audit & Remediation

What the task was

A research team’s shared project directory had drifted out of line with the organisation’s access policy. The job was to read the existing permissions, decide which ones were actually authorised, and correct only what needed correcting.

How the permission string is read

research_team

On a file, x means run it. On a directory it means enter it, which is why removing x rather than r is what actually locked the group out of drafts.

Read the directory, hidden files included
ls -la ~/projects

What changed, and why

FileBefore CommandAfterReason
project_k.txt-rw-rw-rw-chmod o-w-rw-rw-r--Other users could modify the file. Write access for everyone was never authorised.
.project_x.txt-rw--w----chmod ug=r-r--r-----Archived file. User and group read it, nobody writes to it.
drafts/drwx--x---chmod g-xdrwx------Execute on a directory lets you enter it. Removing x was what actually revoked group access.
project_m.txt-rw-r-----chmod g-r-rw-------Already compliant. This change was unnecessary and removed access the team needed.
project_r.txt, project_t.txt-rw-rw-r--none-rw-rw-r--Already met the policy. Left alone.
The changes that were required
chmod o-w  ~/projects/project_k.txt
chmod ug=r ~/projects/.project_x.txt
chmod g-x  ~/projects/drafts

What I got wrong, and what it taught me

  • Removed access that was authorised. Running g-r on project_m.txt stripped read access the team legitimately needed. Over-restricting is a failure too: it stops people doing their job and gets controls worked around.
  • One wrong letter reversed the intent. g+w instead of g+r granted exactly the write access the task existed to prevent.
  • A typo silently did nothing. A misspelled filename returned No such file or directory and changed nothing. Without reading the output I would have recorded it as done.
  • Four commands should have been one. = sets permissions to a known state, while + and - only adjust whatever is already there, so they need the starting state tracked.

Why this sits in a GRC portfolio

Least privilege is a control before it is a command. This is the same work as an access review: establish who is authorised, compare against what is actually granted, correct the difference in both directions, and verify afterwards. The Botium audit found the identical gap at policy level, absent least privilege and separation of duties. This is what closing it looks like on a host.

Five labs from the Google Cybersecurity Certificate (Course 4, Tools of the Trade), run in MariaDB against an organization database. The security team needed answers from three tables: which devices were due a patch, which logins looked suspicious, and which employees sat in the departments and buildings that had problems. What I got wrong, and what it taught me

  • One character dropped 40 records. > '2022-05-09' returned 125 rows and >= returned 165. The difference was every login on the day of the incident. A boundary is a decision, not a detail.
  • An exact match quietly hid data. In Lab 1, country = 'USA' returned 41 rows and skipped anything stored as US. No error, just missing records. I now check the real values before filtering on them.
  • SQL doesn’t read English. department = 'Finance' OR 'Sales' doesn’t mean what it says. The column has to be repeated for each condition, and AND there returns zero rows because nobody is in both departments.
  • Wildcards need LIKE. office = 'South%' looks for an office literally called “South%”. And a missing ; threw ERROR 1064 because the prompt had joined two lines into one query.
  • I mixed up left and right. For the LEFT JOIN question I answered areyes, which is the last row of the RIGHT JOIN. The table after FROM is the left one, so the left join kept every machine and ended on an unassigned one with a NULL username.

Why this sits in a GRC portfolio

Two South building employees, lrodriqu and ejones, have no device on record. An alert sent by device would never reach them. That is an asset inventory gap, the same finding the Botium and Meridian audits raised at policy level, surfaced here from the data itself. Joining employees to the login log took it further: lrodriqu made a failed login, and yappiah, another account with no device, failed twice from Mexico, once after hours. Audit evidence is only as good as the query behind it, and the inconsistent country values show how easily a clean-looking result can be incomplete.
